---
name: earnest
tagline: Short declaratives, plain nouns, feeling left out of the sentence
era: 1920s American
do_not_name: Ernest Hemingway
corpus: 201,000 words measured -- The Sun Also Rises (1926), A Farewell to Arms (1929), Men Without Women (1927), in our time (1924)
---

Every budget below is measured against the corpus named in the frontmatter, not
guessed. Rates are given per 1,000 words so they can be checked against a
chapter without arithmetic. Where the popular impression of this voice and the
measurement disagree, the measurement wins — the impression is a parody, and
writing to it produces prose the corpus does not contain.

## Sentences

- Median sentence **7 words**. Mean 9 to 10. Roughly **70% of sentences run to
  10 words or fewer**, and **a third run to 5 or fewer**.
- Subject, verb, object. One idea per sentence. The sentence opens on its
  grammatical subject unless it opens on a time or place phrase.
- **About 1 sentence per 1,000 words runs past 40.** It is built by chaining
  with `and`, not by nesting: *"...and we crossed the river in August and lived
  in a house that had a fountain and many thick shady trees in a walled garden."*
  Under 1 per 1,000 is fine. Three in a chapter is wrong.
- **At most 6 sentences in 100 carry two or more `and`s.** Chaining is the
  special occasion, not the default texture.
- Relative clauses with `that` are ordinary and frequent — do not avoid them.
- **`which`: at most 2 per chapter.** **`although`, `however`, `therefore`,
  `moreover`, `whereas`: zero.** **`having` + past participle: zero.**
- `because` is allowed, up to **1 per 1,000 words**. It is not a banned word.
- **Semicolons: at most 1 per 1,000 words.** Not zero. **Em dashes: at most 1
  per 1,000 words.** Not zero.
- Active voice. The actor does the thing.

## Words

- Concrete nouns, called by their names: `the boat`, `the rope`, `the wine`.
  Not `the vessel`, `the line`, `the beverage`.
- Short Germanic word over the Latinate one: `get` not `obtain`, `use` not
  `utilize`, `start` not `commence`, `dead` not `deceased`. **Zero occurrences
  of `utilize`, `obtain`, `commence`, `purchase`, `depart`, `reside`.**
- Repeat a key noun rather than reaching for a synonym. Never write `the elderly
  fisherman` to avoid writing `the old man` twice.
- No inflated phrasing: `twelve miles`, not `a distance of twelve miles`.
- **`-ly` adverbs: up to 5 per 1,000 words.** They are not banned and they are
  not rare — the corpus runs about 5 per 1,000, the same as ordinary prose.
  What is banned is what they attach to. In **narration**, an `-ly` adverb may
  modify a verb (`walked slowly`, `looked carefully`, `finally stopped`) and may
  **never modify an adjective** (`awfully cold`, `terribly good`). In
  **dialogue**, no restriction.
- **Rating adjectives — `beautiful`, `splendid`, `gorgeous`, `magnificent`,
  `terrible`, `wonderful`, `dreadful`, `lovely`, `awful` — are banned in
  narration and unrestricted in dialogue.** Seven in ten of these belong to
  characters. People of this era say `awfully nice` constantly; the narrator
  never does.
- Adjectives in narration give information, not a rating: `cold`, `wet`,
  `brown`, `empty`, `flat`, `gray`.
- **`suddenly`: at most 1 per 2,000 words.**
- Definite articles run **6.5 to 7.5 per 100 words** — noticeably heavy. A thing
  is `the` thing, as though the reader already knew it.

## Emotion and meaning

- **Do not name an emotion more than twice per 1,000 words**, and never at the
  moment it is largest. Show the hand that shakes, the drink poured and not
  drunk, the thing left unsaid. The corpus names emotions about twice per 1,000
  words, so the rule is a ration, not a prohibition.
- **Zero instances of `he realized`, `she understood`, `it occurred to him`,
  `he knew then`, `she felt a wave of`.**
- No sentence explains why a character did something.
- No narrator comment on an event after the event. No moralizing, no summary of
  what the reader should take from a scene.
- Leave out the thing the scene is about. What is omitted must still be known to
  the writer, so the omission has a shape.
- **When the event is largest, the sentence gets shorter.** The chapter's
  heaviest moment is carried by a sentence below the median of 7 words.

## Description

- Facts in the order the eye and ear take them in. Position, then object, then
  what it is doing.
- Describe only what a camera and a microphone in the scene could record.
  Temperature, light, distance, the taste of the thing, the sound it made.
- Do not interpret a landscape. `The wind came off the water and it was cold`,
  not `the air was pleasant`.
- Name distances, prices, drinks, and places exactly. Numbers are specific.
- **Similes: up to 1 per 1,000 words, and both terms must be physical objects.**
  `like a` and `as ... as` are in the corpus at about that rate. No metaphor
  that calls attention to itself, no cliché, no comparison to an abstraction.
- Colours and objects are given once, plainly, and let alone.

## Dialogue

- **`said` and `asked` are the only tags.** Measured: `said` is 86–88% of tags,
  `asked` is 10%, everything else is under 4% and can go. No `exclaimed`,
  `murmured`, `retorted`, `replied`, `answered`, `shouted`.
- **Zero tags carrying an adverb.** Across 201,000 words there are fourteen. Use
  none.
- Drop the tag entirely wherever alternation makes the speaker clear.
- No stage direction inside a tag. Not `he said, running a hand through his
  hair`. The action gets its own sentence.
- Characters do not explain themselves. They deflect, repeat, answer a different
  question, or go quiet.
- Repetition inside dialogue is the point: a line said twice, a word thrown back.
- **No dialect spelling.** Register is carried by word choice and syntax.
- **No speech longer than 60 words without an interruption.**

## Rhythm

- **Paragraphs run to a median of 2 sentences.** Mean under 3.
- **35 to 40 out of every 100 paragraphs are a single sentence.** This is the
  ordinary texture, not a device saved for moments of weight.
- The `and` chain carries motion and fatigue; the short sentence stops it. Put
  them next to each other on purpose.
- A paragraph does not end on an abstract noun.
- **A scene ends on a concrete image or a flat line of dialogue.** Never on
  reflection, never on a summary, never on the narrator's verdict.

## Before / after

**Before**

> Overwhelmed by a profound sense of loss, Maria gazed out at the shimmering,
> endless ocean, which seemed to mirror her own turbulent emotions, and she
> realized with sudden clarity that nothing would ever be the same.

**After**

> Maria stood at the rail and looked at the water. It was flat and gray and went
> out to where the sky came down. She held the rail a long time. Then she went
> below and did not come up again that day.

Forty-two words become forty-five. The voice is not shorter overall — the
sentences are shorter and there are more of them. Nothing that happened has been
cut: she is at the rail, she looks at the sea, she is changed, she leaves. What
is gone is the naming of the loss, the rating of the ocean, the `which` clause,
and the narrator's verdict at the end.

## Checklist

Run every item against the finished draft. Quote the offending sentence for each
`no`, fix it, and run the list again.

1. Is the median sentence length 7 words or fewer, and are ~70% of sentences
   10 words or fewer?
2. Are there more than 1 sentence per 1,000 words running past 40 words?
3. Does `which` appear more than twice, or `although`, `however`, `therefore`,
   `moreover`, or `having` + past participle appear at all?
4. Do `-ly` adverbs exceed 5 per 1,000 words, and does any one modify an
   adjective in narration?
5. Does any dialogue tag other than `said` or `asked` appear?
6. Does any tag carry an adverb, or contain a stage direction?
7. Does a rating adjective (`beautiful`, `terrible`, `lovely`, `wonderful`,
   `splendid`, `awful`) appear outside dialogue?
8. Are emotions named more than twice per 1,000 words, or named at the scene's
   heaviest moment?
9. Does `he realized`, `she understood`, `it occurred to him`, or `she felt a
   wave of` appear anywhere?
10. Does any sentence explain a character's motive, or comment on an event after
    it?
11. Do similes exceed 1 per 1,000 words, or does any compare a physical thing to
    an abstraction?
12. Does any paragraph end on an abstract noun, or any scene end on reflection?
13. Are 35–40% of paragraphs a single sentence?
14. Is the `do_not_name` value anywhere in the draft?
15. Does the draft contain a scene, character, or joke absent from the source, or
    is any named character from the source chapter missing?
16. Does the draft describe or explain its own style?
